> *The Privacy and Other Legislative Amendment Act 2024* (Cth)

###### OVERVIEW

> On 29 November 2024 the *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill* 2024 (Cth) passed both Houses of Parliament and on 10 December 2024 received Royal Assent. Subsequently, the ***Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024* (Cth)** is now in effect.
>
> The Act has substantially changed the Australian privacy regime with key changes including:

- Statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy;

- Criminalisation of doxxing;

- Disclosure requirement for automated decision-making;

- OAIC to develop a Children's Online Privacy Code;

- Ministerial powers to 'whitelist' countries that provide similar privacy protections;

- The OAIC new powers to issue infringement and compliance notices;

- Requirement for 'reasonable steps' to be taken to protect the security of personal information.

## Doxxing

> Unlike the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, doxxing was not one of the 116 recommendations in the 2022 Privacy Act Review Report. Instead, the Commonwealth Government sought to accelerate reform to criminalise doxxing following the February 2024 leak of a private WhatsApp group of more than 600 members of the Australian Jewish community. Personal details of names, professions, photos and social media profiles were exposed, leading to harassment, threats and some individuals going into hiding. The Commonwealth Government conducted a public consultation in March 2024 and in September 2024, doxxing was included in the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2024 (Cth).

###### WHAT IS DOXXING?

> "Doxxing" or "doxing" derived from "dropping documents" refers to the publishing of someone's personal data on the internet, usually with malicious intent. A prominent incident occurred in 2015 when the global dating website *Ashley Madison* was hacked by the "Impact Team." The attackers threatened to release user data unless the site shut down; when it did not, the personal details of millions of users were published online.
>
> The eSafety Commissioner published a position statement^1^ recognising three main forms of doxxing:

- **De-anonymizing doxxing** - revealing the identity of a previously anonymous person.

- **Targeting doxxing** - revealing specific information that allows a person to be contacted, located or impersonated.

- **De-legitimizing doxxing** - revealing sensitive or intimate information about a person (e.g. medical, financial or legal records).

###### PRIOR LEGAL AVENUES

> Before 2024, Australia had no standalone offence for doxxing. Instead, a patchwork of existing laws could be applicable for the conduct of doxxing, including:

- Using a carriage service to menace, harass or offend under section 474.17 of the

> *Criminal Code Act 1995* (Cth).

- Identification information intending to commit or facilitate an indictable offence under the *Crimes Act 1900* (Cth)*.*

- State stalking and intimidation laws (e.g. in NSW, intimidation is criminalised under section 13 of the *Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence)* Act 2007).

- Civil claims, such as defamation or breach of confidence.

> Doxxing was criminalised as the current measures were regarded as limited in scope, carried lower penalties and did not encourage prosecution by law enforcement officers.

###### NEW OFFENCES UNDER THE CRIMINAL CODE

> The *Privacy and Other Legislative Amendment Act* 2024 (Cth) has criminalised doxxing by inserting sections 474.17C and 474.17D into the *Criminal Code Act 1995* (Cth):

- **section 474.17C** criminalises making available or publishing personal data of one or more individuals, and

- **section 474.17D** criminalises making available or publishing personal data of one or more members of a group.

> The maximum penalty for doxxing against an individual is 6 years' imprisonment and if the doxxing is against a person because they are a member of a group the maximum penalty increases to 7 years' imprisonment.
>
> The elements that need to be proved beyond reasonable doubt are as follows:

- The accused used a carriage service (internet, phone, social media etc.) to make available, publish or distribute information, and

- The information was personal data, and

- A reasonable person would regard the conduct as menacing or harassing.

> For section 474.17D only, it must also be satisfied that the conduct was wholly or partly due to the accused's belief that the member was distinguishable by race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status, disability, nationality or national or ethnic origin. \[NOTE: It is immaterial whether the group is actually distinguished by the listed\].
>
> "Personal data" has been defined more broadly than personal information under the Privacy Act to include any data that allows an individual to be identified, contacted or located. A
>
> non-exhaustive list is contained in sections 474.17C(2) and 474.17D(2) which details: names, photograph/images, telephone numbers, email address, online accounts, residential address, work or business address, place of education and places of worship.
>
> By including the doxxing offences in the Criminal Code, they are not subject to the exemptions under the Privacy Act, meaning small businesses and journalists are not exempt
>
> and could be charged with committing a doxxing offence. Additionally, by including the doxxing offences in the Criminal Code, a corporation can be found liable as well as individuals.

###### OTHER JURISDICTIONS

> Other jurisdictions take varied approaches to doxxing.
>
> In the UK, there is no standalone law prohibiting doxxing but the conduct may breach the *Data Protection Act 2018, Malicious Communications Act 1988* or the *Protection from Harassment Act 1997.*
>
> Additionally, in the US, there are no state laws prohibiting doxxing but perpetrators may be prosecuted under anti-intimidation laws not protected under the First Amendment which protects the right to publish newsworthy information.

###### ENFORCEMENT AND OTHER CHALLENGES

> The effectiveness of the doxxing offences will depend on their enforcement. Additionally, significant challenges are likely to remain such as jurisdictional limitations with perpetrators residing overseas, anonymity with perpetrators concealing their identity and legislative thresholds as the conduct must meet the criteria of being "menacing" or "harassing."
>
> The Act requires the Minister to undertake an independent review of the anti-doxxing measures within 24 months of commencement. The effectiveness of the new doxxing laws to prosecute perpetrators and deter harmful conduct will likely be a subject of legislative review in the near future.
