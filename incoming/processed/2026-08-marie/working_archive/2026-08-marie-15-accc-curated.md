## The ACCC's news bargaining code

The _News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code_ was the first legislative scheme of its kind. It differs from most of the material in this chapter: its subject is not intermediary liability for content, but the allocation of bargaining power between digital platforms and news publishers.

### Background

The Code originated in the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission's Digital Platforms Inquiry, which reported in 2019.[^nmbc_dpi] The inquiry found that the market position of Google in search and Meta in social media produced a bargaining imbalance in their dealings with Australian news businesses: news organisations bear the cost of producing journalism that contributes to engagement on those services, while having little leverage to negotiate payment for it. The ACCC recommended a binding code of conduct.

### How the Code operates

The Code was enacted by the _Treasury Laws Amendment (News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code) Act 2021_ (Cth).[^nmbc_act] It does not apply automatically. The Treasurer may 'designate' a digital platform where satisfied that a bargaining power imbalance exists and that the platform has not made a significant contribution to the sustainability of the Australian news industry.

Once designated, a platform must negotiate in good faith with eligible news businesses over payment for news content made available on its services. Where the parties cannot agree within a set period, the matter proceeds to compulsory final offer arbitration: each side submits a final offer, and an independent panel must select one of them in its entirety, without splitting the difference.

The design of that mechanism is the point of interest. Because the panel cannot moderate an extreme offer, each party has an incentive to submit the most reasonable offer it can, in order to be the one selected. The mechanism is intended to operate in the shadow of the negotiation rather than to be used.

### Outcomes

No platform has been designated. The prospect of designation, and of the arbitration that would follow, was sufficient to bring the major platforms to negotiate: Google and Meta entered commercial agreements with a range of Australian news organisations, from the largest publishers to smaller independent outlets.[^nmbc_sims] This is the sense in which the Code has been described as operating in the shadow of the law — its effects have been produced almost entirely by the threat of its operation rather than by its application.[^nmbc_mcintyre]

The response was not uniform. In February 2021, while the Bill was before Parliament, Meta restricted the sharing and viewing of Australian news content on Facebook, a restriction it reversed after amendments were agreed. Meta has since indicated that it does not intend to renew its Australian agreements, which raises the question of what the Code can achieve against a platform that is willing to withdraw from carrying news altogether.

### Assessment and influence

The model has been followed elsewhere; Canada enacted the _Online News Act_ in 2023, and comparable proposals have been considered in other jurisdictions.[^nmbc_canada]

Two criticisms recur. The first is distributional: the benefits are said to accrue disproportionately to large established publishers, which are best placed to negotiate, rather than to the smaller and independent outlets whose sustainability was part of the rationale. The second is transparency: because the agreements are commercial and confidential, neither the amounts nor the conditions attached to them are public, which makes it difficult to assess whether the scheme has in fact supported public interest journalism.[^nmbc_mcintyre]
