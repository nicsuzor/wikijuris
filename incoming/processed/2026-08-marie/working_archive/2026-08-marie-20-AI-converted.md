**Author's rights**

Local authors are currently calling on the Australian government to create dedicated AI copyright laws. Copyright fees are a main source of income for Australian writers and 79% said they would not allow their existing work to be used to train AI models, even if they were paid. [^1]

In November 2024, the Australian Senate Select Committee on AI advised the government to mandate transparency from AI developers regarding the use of copyrighted materials in their training datasets. They also recommended that such usage be properly licensed and compensated.

Subsequently, in August 2025, the Productivity Commission released its interim report *Harnessing Data and Digital Technology*, proposing the introduction of a text and data mining exception to the Copyright Act. This exception would operate either as a new fair dealing provision or as a broader fair use defence, permitting the use of copyrighted works for the purpose of training AI systems under specified conditions. The Commission argued that such a reform could unlock significant economic value, estimated at \$116 billion over the next decade, by facilitating innovation and productivity growth in the AI sector. Clearly, this suggestion has sparked backlash from authors, who fear it could undermine the value of their copyright protections.

If adopted, the proposed text and data mining exception to the *Copyright Act* would enable technology companies to utilise copyrighted works for AI training purposes; potentially granting access to content that was previously was not on the open internet.

**Current Legislation/Remedies**

The foundations of copyright law in Australia are in the *Copyright Act* 1968 (Cth), which grants authors the exclusive rights over their original works. These rights include:

- Reproduction: Making copies of the work, even temporary or digital ones.

<!-- -->

- Communication to the public: Sharing the work via the internet, broadcast, or other public channels.

<!-- -->

- Adaptation: Creating derivative works, such as translations or dramatizations.

Under Section 21 of the Act, reproduction is broadly defined. It includes:

- Digital conversion

<!-- -->

- Temporary copies: Even short-term digital copies e.g. cached files can count

These rights should give authors substantive protection; however, the current legal landscape offers limited scope for copyright to be effectively enforced against AI. This is shown by the lack of current compensation for authors. Although, the enforceability of reproduction is yet to be substantially challenged in Australia. The *Copyright Act* protections generally require a human author who contributes skill and judgment, meaning that works created autonomously by AI are not automatically covered. While the Australian Government is actively considering the introduction of a dedicated legislative framework to regulate AI and make legal issues clearer, they are also conscious to balance the needs of the consumer and the innovative solutions that AI can offer.

Adding further consideration to the copyright provisions, notably, section 116AC of the *Copyright Act* provides that:

"A service provider carries out a Category A activity by providing facilities or services for transmitting, routing or providing connections for copyright material, or the intermediate and transient storage of copyright material in the course of transmission, routing or provision of connections."[^2]

Although this provision is primarily intended to regulate the conduct of internet service providers, its application arguably extends to AI systems. Specifically, AI platforms that facilitate the transmission or temporary storage of copyright-protected content, whether intentionally or automated , may be construed as engaging in activities like those described in section 116AC. Accordingly, there remains a potential avenue for legal argument that AI technologies, depending on their function and deployment, could fall within the scope of intermediary liability or contributory infringement under Australian copyright law.

Under Australian law, intermediary liability is shaped by the doctrine of "authorisation", found in sections 36(1) and 101(1) of the Copyright Act.[^3] These provisions establish that a person infringes copyright not only by directly engaging in infringing acts, but also by authorising others to do so. The High Court's decision in *University of New South Wales v Moorhouse* shows that by providing facilities that enable infringement without taking reasonable steps to prevent it may amount to authorisation.[^4] Additionally, contributory infringement requires knowledge that the infringement is occurring and material contribution which both are present here.

While no Australian case has yet explored the extent of copyright protection and AI, Sections 115-116 of the *Copyright Act* offer general remedies, alongside protections under contract and consumer law, should an author decide to pursue action against AI usage. These sections of the *Copyright Act* encompass the rights of the authors under copyright. In the US *Reuters* has shown that "wholesale copying" for AI training could constitute copyright infringement, suggesting the possibility of similar claims in Australia.[^5]

Below are some examples of potential remedies for breach of copyright in the AI sector:

a.  Copyright infringement claims

- If an AI model reproduces a substantial part of a copyrighted work, the copyright holder could sue for infringement under S116 of the *Copyright Act*. See earlier in the text for the two elements of infringement.[^6]

<!-- -->

- Remedies may include injunctions (to stop use), damages, or an account of profits. [^7]

- The importance of the market value, proportion of the article that infringes copyright and the extent to which the material can be separated will be considered.[^8]

<!-- -->

- Many AI systems learn from data in ways that don't copy works directly but use them in training, making infringement harder to prove without software accountability.

  a.  Contractual remedies

<!-- -->

- Some platforms prohibit scraping or unauthorised use in their terms of service

- If AI companies breach these terms by using the data inappropriately, the remedy could be contractual damages or injunctions. Contractual damages aim to put the client in the same situation that they were originally.

- Negligence could be used as a tort. There is a foreseeable duty of care that the writer would suffer a loss, the harm to the writer would not have occurred but for the misuse of their product and the plaintiff has suffered clear economic loss as the individual would have had to pay for the work but for AI.[^9]

  a.  Confidential information

<!-- -->

- If AI developers use non-public or confidential material (e.g. subscription databases, proprietary datasets etc.) without consent, the owner could sue for breach of confidence or misappropriation, leading to penalties similar to the infringement claims ones.

- For the information to be misused without authority to the detriment of the confider, it must not be readily available on the internet (*Coco* case).[^10]

  a.  Moral rights

<!-- -->

- Authors have rights to attribution and against false attribution. If AI reproduces or manipulates works without credit, authors may argue a breach of moral rights, though this remains untested.

The above provisions for legal claims and protections will continue to be explored and refined in the years to come.
