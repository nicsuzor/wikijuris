**15. Regulation and Cautions Regarding Cryptocurrency**

A cryptocurrency is classified as a digital asset, whose main purpose is to provide a secured, online medium of exchange which is controlled by its own system. A simplified classification of cryptocurrencies is to say that they are a subset of digital currencies. Over recent years there has been an astronomical rise in the value and number of cryptocurrencies available, with some scholars dubbing cryptocurrency as the biggest technological invention in the last ten years.[^1]

One of the major driving forces behind the rise of cryptocurrencies is that the transactions occur through the blockchain system, meaning that regulation and control of the cryptocurrency is not granted to any one governing body. The appeal behind this method of currency exchange is that it prevents governing bodies from producing new units (which leads to inflation) and therefore protects the value of each individual unit in the process. Additionally, by cryptocurrencies having their own unique blockchain system it prevents surveillance on any given transaction, protecting the privacy of individuals who are performing trades using cryptocurrencies.[^2]

**a. Exploitation of Cryptocurrencies for Illegal or Immoral Activities**

By providing users with a blockchain system of exchange, the emergence of cryptocurrencies has inadvertently resulted in several negative consequences, most notably being (1) the facilitation of illegal activities and (2) a major rise in "pump and dump" schemes ('P&D').[^3]

**aa. Facilitation of Illegal Activities**

Studies have found that cryptocurrencies have been used in relation to several illegal activities including illegal trade and services (drugs, firearms, pornography, murder-for-hire), potential to fund terrorism, launder money and avoid capital controls.[^4] A recent study found that approximately one-quarter of bitcoin users (26%) and almost one-half of bitcoin transactions (46%) are related to illegal activity, which amounted to a total of \$76 billion being spent on illegal activity per year through bitcoin.[^5] A major marketplace that was used to facilitate illegal trade and services while utilising cryptocurrencies was Silk Road, until it was shut down in 2013 by the FBI's infiltration and seizure of the website.[^6]

While governing bodies were able to identify that a total of \$9 billion in transactions occurred across a number of contraband sites, the governing bodies were only able to effectively identify 1% of the dollar value of transactions as relating to contraband. A contributing factor to governing bodies inability to effectively track transactions on these contraband websites is due to systems like CoinJoin, which mixes balances of cryptocurrencies from different addresses and sends them to new addresses, effectively disguising the origin of the funds.[^7]

Another factor preventing effective policing of cryptocurrency transactions is due to the emergence of "shadow" cryptocurrencies, which are opaquer and better at concealing a user's activity.[^8] While these factors do make it more difficult for governing bodies to regulate cryptocurrencies, the FBI's seizure of the Silk Road marketplace creates opportunities for future potential seizures which would have a deterrent effect on continued illegal trade.

**ab. Major Rise in "Pump and Dump" Schemes**

A P&D is a form of price manipulation where an asset's price is artificially inflated before the cheaply purchased asset is sold at a higher price. This issue with these schemes is that once the assets are "dumped", the price falls drastically and investors lose money. Historically, these schemes have been limited to microcap stocks, which can be regulated by governing bodies such as ASIC (Australian Securities and Investments Commission) who have the power to punish those performing P&D schemes under the *ASIC Market Integrity Rules (Securities Market) 2017* (Cth) and make fines of up to \$1,000,000.[^9]

However, with the emergence of cryptocurrencies and the blockchain system of exchange, governing bodies such as ASIC do not have access to enough information to effectively investigate and punish those responsible for P&D schemes, allowing for perpetrators of P&D schemes to operate without punishment. Another contributing factor relating to the rise of P&D schemes in the cryptocurrency sphere is that cryptocurrency P&Ds typically last only for several minutes, whereas a stock market P&D lasts for months, allowing for P&D schemes to operate more frequently through cryptocurrencies.[^10]

Studies have shown that internal regulation against P&D schemes by certain cryptocurrencies has been effective in reducing the overall prevalence of P&D schemes for those cryptocurrencies when compared to others. A case study was conducted in relation to two cryptocurrencies, Bittrex and Yobit, whereby Bittrex internally regulated and banned P&Ds, whereas Yobit encouraged P&D schemes. The study found that Bittrex's ban resulted in increased prices and volumes of cryptocurrencies listed on the exchange compared to ordinary cryptocurrencies, and that Yobit's encouragement resulted in decreased prices and volumes of cryptocurrencies comparative to ordinary cryptocurrencies.[^11] This study indicates that P&Ds are harmful to the effectiveness of cryptocurrencies, and suggests that the best way for regulation of the cryptocurrency sector to occur is through internal governing and rules established by each cryptocurrency.

**b. Combatting Cryptocurrency Exploitation**

Nations around the world have struggled to come up with effective measures to prevent the exploitation of cryptocurrencies for illegal or immoral activities. In the United States, there was a proposal for the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to implement a \$100,000,000 cryptocurrency exchange-traded fund (ETF) which would allow US citizens to invest in a cryptocurrency that was owned by the government, however, the SEC rejected the proposal due to concerns regarding a lack of regulation once it had been implemented, due to the nature of the blockchain system that cryptocurrencies use.[^12] In China, the government banned residents from trading in cryptocurrencies altogether, which has been effective in preventing cryptocurrency abuse.

The leading approach which seems to be favoured by many countries was pioneered by Singapore and Switzerland, whereby they incorporated cryptocurrency- related business into existing regulatory frameworks, which has been followed in Australia, whereby ASIC now has the power to enforce laws relating to crypto assets which fall within the definition of a 'financial product'.[^13] This integration has allowed Australian laws to punish those responsible for certain offences relating to illegal cryptocurrency use, however, there are still major issues regarding the gathering of information relating to these offences due to the anonymity of cryptocurrency users.[^14]

While studies have indicated that creating and regulating a government-backed cryptocurrency could be an effective way of combatting cryptocurrency exploitation, central bank representatives have warned against the idea, due to issues regarding control and influence in relation to steering the economy, and statistical agencies wont be able to collect data about economic activity due to the anonymous nature of blockchain trading.[^15]
