# THE HIGH COURT'S REJECTION OF 'INSTRUMENTAL' PUBLICATION: THE POST-DEFTEROS ERA

**Introduction**

The High Court of Australia's decision in the case of *Google LLC v Defteros* \[2022\] HCA 27 is a pivotal moment in the governance of online speech in Australia, specifically concerning the doctrine of publication in relation to search engines. The High Court, in a 5-2 majority, overturned the previous rulings from the Supreme Court of Victoria and determined that Google was not a "publisher" of a defamatory article to which its search results linked to. This judgement represents a significant shift in relation to legal interpretation, moving from a broad "instrumentalist" approach towards a narrower and limited "apathetic conception" of their role.

This decision made by the High Court demonstrates stark contrast to the court's decision in *Fairfax Media Publications Pty Ltd v Voller* \[2021\] HCA 27, in which the media companies were found to be publishers of third-party comments on their Facebook pages because they were seen as "facilitating, encouraging, and assisting" the publication of that content. In the case of *Defteros,* the High Court decided that the holding that a search results has is merely a "facilitating tool" that provides access to information, instead of a direct participation in its publication.

While the decision provides necessary clarity for digital platform, it also raised questions regarding the existing common law of defamation and its adequacy to address unique forms of harm developing in the digital environment.

**The doctrine of publication, it's re-evaluation**

The common law tort of defamation requires the plaintiff to prove three elements:

1.  The material must be defamatory;

2.  The plaintiff must be identified; and

3.  It must be published.

The decision in *Defteros* centred on the element of 'publication', finding that Google's conduct did not constitute the act of publication.

While the Victorian Court of Appeal had adopted a broad, "instrumentalist" view -- holding that the provision of a hyperlink was an act that "lent assistance to the publication" of the underlying article -- the High Court rejected this reasoning. The majority, in a series of judgments, concluded that the act of providing a hyperlink is a fundamentally different function from the act of publishing content. As Chief Justice Kiefel and Justice Gleeson articulated, a hyperlink is merely a *"content-neutral"* reference that directs a user to a separate webpage.[^1]

The High Court demonstrated a legal distinction between participating in a defamatory communication and a merely facilitating access to it. The majority held that Google was a passive tool, not an active participant in the bilateral process of communication. The search engine's algorithms simply identified and ranked content that had already existed on the internet in response to the user's query. The act was analogous to a librarian giving a list of books on a particular topic -- the librarian is not responsible for the contents of the books, nor do they "publish" the books by providing the list. The responsibility remains with the original author and primary publisher. The defamatory content was not made available for comprehension by Google's act alone; it required the subsequent and independent action of a user clicking the link to access the material. The legal reasoning was fortified by the Canadian Supreme Court's decision in *Crookes v Newton* \[2011\] 3 SCR 269. It held that a hyperlink does not, in itself, constitutes publication.[^2]

**Distinguishing *Defteros* from *Voller***

High Court's decision in *Google v Defteros* is best understood in light of its preceding judgement in *Fairfax Media Publications Pty Ltd v Voller* \[2021\] HCA 27. It is important to distinguish between the two cases and their legal precedents.

In *Voller,* the High Court found that media companies were "publishers" of defamatory third-party comments on their Facebook pages and profiles. The court's reasoning was that creating and maintaining a public forum for discussion and encouraging comments, the media companies have "*facilitated, encourages, and thereby assisted"*[^3] the publication of the comments. This was determined to be a voluntary act of participation in the communication of defamatory material.

In *Defteros,* Google's search engine was an automated system designed to index the entire web. Its function was to provide a list of search results in response to a user-initiated query. The Court found that it was not a deliberate act of encouragement or enticement of any specific defamatory content.

The juxtaposition clarifies the High Court's approach in which the key lies in the level of control and intentionality. In the case of *Voller* the act of setting up and operating the Facebook page was direct and intentional acts to facilitate communication. Whereas in *Defteros* providing a hyperlink was viewed as a passive and automated process that merely provides information about the existence and location on content, published elsewhere on the internet.

The High Court concluded that where an entity creates a platform for user-generated content and exercises some form of control over it, they are more likely to be considered a publisher. Where the entity\'s role is merely to provide a reference or a tool to locate pre-existing content, liability for publication is unlikely to attach.[^4]

**Ongoing Implications**

The decision in *Defteros* applies specifically to "organic" search and does not provide a definitive answer on liability for other forms of content such as defamatory snippets that appear directly in search results or sponsored links.[^5] Commentators have noted, the context and nature of a search result could still be critical.[^6]

Moreover, the judgement provided, reinforces the strict liability nature of defamation law and redirects the burden of proof back to the plaintiffs to pursue the original publisher of the content, which can particularly challenging when the original creator is anonymous or located in foreign jurisdiction.[^7]

**Conclusion**

The High Court's ruling represents a significant jurisprudential development which offers a constrained and precise application of law of defamation to the complexities of the digital environment. The judgement clarifies the provision of a hyperlink, in and of itself, is not an act of publication, which also aligns Australian jurisprudence with international precedent, especially the Canadian Supreme Court's reasoning in *Crookes v Newton.*

However, the ruling is not without its critics. It has been argued that while it resolves the narrow issue of hyperlinks, it leaves broader questions unresolved, such as liability for defamatory snippets or sponsored links. Furthermore, by absolving search engines of responsibility, the decision places the considerable burden of litigation on the defamed party, who must often pursue the original content creator, a task fraught with practical and jurisdictional difficulties. Ultimately, while the Defteros case offers a measure of certainty for a specific category of online intermediaries, it simultaneously highlights the challenges of applying traditional common law to the complexities of digital communication.
