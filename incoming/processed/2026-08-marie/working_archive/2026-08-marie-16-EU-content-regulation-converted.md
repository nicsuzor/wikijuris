**Content Regulation in the European Union -- The Digital Services Act**

The European Union has updated their legal framework for regulating online content with the *Digital Services Act* ("DSA"), which entered into force in 2022 and became fully applicable in February 2024. The *DSA* is the EU's most ambitious attempt to harmonise platform regulation across multiple jurisdictions, establishing a single set of rules that apply directly to providers of intermediary services, online platforms, and search engines operating within the EU market.

**Objectives**

The *DSA* seeks to update the EU's legal framework for dealing with online intermediaries to better address the systemic risks arising from the dissemination of harmful or illegal content. It creates a tiered system of obligations, with basic duties applying to all providers of intermediary services and progressively more demanding requirements imposed on Very Large Online Platforms ("VLOPs") and Very Large Online Search Engines ("VLOSEs"), defined as those with more than 45 million monthly users in the EU (10% of the EU's population). The European Commission directly supervises compliance for VLOPs and VLOSEs, while national regulators known as Digital Services Coordinators oversee other providers.

**Obligations Imposed**

The obligations introduced by the *DSA* are wide-ranging. All intermediary service providers are required to maintain transparency in their terms of service and to establish "notice and action" mechanisms enabling users to flag illegal content. Online platforms must provide internal complaint handling systems, cooperate with "trusted flaggers," and suspend accounts that repeatedly disseminate manifestly illegal content. They are also required to increase transparency around online advertising. This includes mandatory labelling of sponsored content and prohibitions on targeted advertising directed at children or based on sensitive categories such as political opinion or sexual orientation.

For VLOPs and VLOSEs, the regulatory demands are more extensive. Unlike smaller intermediaries, these platforms must conduct annual risk assessments to identify and mitigate systemic risks associated with the dissemination of disinformation, electoral manipulation, gender-based violence, and other online harms. Mitigation measures may include making algorithmic changes, restrictions on advertising practices, or enhanced cooperation with fact-checkers. The *DSA* also introduces an unprecedented degree of scrutiny by requiring independent audits of these platforms' compliance and mandating that accredited researchers studying systemic risks have access to datasets. These measures are intended to manage the significant influence VLOPs and VLOSEs have over democratic discourse, public health and the protection of minors.

**Enforcement**

Enforcement of the *DSA* is backed by strong sanctions. The European Commission has the authority to impose fines of up to six per cent of a company's global annual turnover for non-compliance, while persistent and serious infringements may even result in a temporary suspension of services within the EU, although this has not yet occurred.

**Impact of the *DSA***

The introduction of the *DSA* has been an example of the "*Brussels effect*," where EU regulation sets global standards by virtue of its economic weight. Many large technology companies have announced that they will apply *DSA* compliance measures beyond the EU as a cost saving measure, which means that European regulatory norms will essentially be exported to other jurisdictions. this worldwide impact underscores the DSA's significance as not merely a regional initiative but a global benchmark in platform regulation.

**Reactions to the *DSA***

Critics of the *DSA* have raised concerns about its potential impact on freedom of expression. Some argue that obligations requiring platforms to act swiftly against illegal content may incentivise excessive censorship. This sentiment has been particularly echoed by large American tech companies, who were the subject of 60 enforcement actions just in the first year after the *DSA's* entry into force.

There is also concern over the feasibility of implementing effective risk-mitigation measures without creating undue burdens on smaller platforms. The European Commission has attempted to address this issue by offering targeted 12-month exemptions to companies transitioning into VLOPs or VLOSEs. Nonetheless, the *DSA* has been praised by many for introducing a more updated regulatory approach that is more transparent and better addresses the central role VLOPs and VLOSEs play in shaping the online environment.

**Other Relevant Policy Instruments**

The *DSA* does not exist in isolation but operates alongside other EU policy instruments. The *Digital Markets Act* ("DMA"), which also entered into force in 2022, complements the *DSA* by imposing obligations on so-called "gatekeeper" platforms to prevent anti-competitive conduct and preserve contestability in digital markets.

The *Audiovisual Media Services Directive* (AVMSD) introduced in 2010 imposes standards on audiovisual media providers, including video-sharing platforms, with a focus on the protection of minors and the prohibition of incitement to violence or hatred.

The EU also introduced the *Code of Practice on Disinformation* in 2018. It is a voluntary but widely adopted instrument designed to tackle online misinformation through commitments by platforms to improve transparency, curb manipulative advertising, and support independent fact-checking. While voluntary in nature, the Code's principles have been reinforced by the binding obligations introduced in the *DSA*.
