**Introducing a Statutory Right to Privacy (2025)**

In June 2025, changes to the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth)[^1] took effect to introduce a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy.[^2] This offered individuals a formal statutory right to privacy for the first time in Australia. Critics had argued that the existing Act was no longer fit for purpose in a digital era, and not capable of responding adequately to threats such as doxing[^3] and large-scale data breaches. This development was many years in the making, and it took several reviews before the decision was reached.

a)  **Background and Developments**

Until the early 2000s, Australia had been operating on the assumption that there was no general right to privacy, as famously established in *Victoria Park Racing and Recreation Grounds Co Ltd v Taylor* (1937).[^4] However, in *Australian Broadcasting Corporation v Lenah Game Meats Pty Ltd* (2001) the High Court departed from this view, and Callinan J went so far as to indicate that the *Victoria Park* decision was not applicable in a case of invasion of privacy.[^5] This was a significant turning point on the matter, with *Victoria Park* being described previously as an 'obstacle' to Australia's development of a tort for invasion of privacy.[^6]

However, *Lenah Game Meats* stopped short of formally acknowledging such a tort. Subsequent reviews of Australia's privacy laws in the years after the case suggested that statutory reform would therefore be beneficial in clarifying uncertainty in the law.

In its 2008 report, *For Your Information: Australian Privacy Law and Practice*, the Australian Law Reform Commission (ALRC) observed that Australia was trailing behind other jurisdictions in developing statutory torts for invasions of privacy.[^7] It also suggested that overlapping and complex privacy laws across federal, state and territory jurisdictions made enforcement difficult, that privacy principles differed between the private and public sectors, and that definitions in the *Privacy Act* were not well suited to emerging technologies.[^8]

In December 2017, the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) conducted an inquiry into digital platforms,[^9] resulting in the *Digital Platforms Inquiry -- Final Report* in June 2019.[^10] This report highlighted the effects that digital platforms have on society and the responsibilities that the platforms hold in facilitating access to digital technologies. An important consideration was how consumer protection and privacy is handled in an ever-changing digital era. With expansive consumer information now circulating online, it advised of necessary reform to the *Privacy Act* to protect the privacy of consumers. Specifically, Recommendation 19 was to introduce a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy.[^11]

The *Privacy Act Review 2023* subsequently reinforced the need for reform as Australians increasingly lived within the online space and faced risks such as data breaches, identity fraud, blackmail and reputational harm.[^12] Lower courts had recognised a tort of invasion of privacy (*Grosse v Purvis*; *Doe v ABC*)[^13] however appellate courts had not, and the High Court in *Smethurst v Commissioner of Police* \[2020\] acknowledged privacy issues but still left them unresolved. [^14] This reaffirmed that without statutory reform, the matter would likely remain uncertain and jurisdictionally inconsistent.

b)  **Statutory Reform**

The *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2024* was introduced to Parliament on 12 September 2024 and passed by both houses on 10 December 2024. In his Explanatory Memorandum,[^15] Attorney-General Mark Dreyfus addressed many of the issues within the existing *Privacy Act* which had been highlighted by the preceding reports, with several proposals to strengthen the Act. Among these was the introduction of a statutory cause of action in tort for serious invasions of privacy.

The Explanatory Memorandum outlined that reforms needed to be flexible so it could address 'current and emerging privacy risks,' and to expand the types of serious invasions of privacy, such as physical privacy and misuse of information. It also pointed to recent technological developments which could impact upon an individual's right to privacy, including (but not limited to) doxing.

One particular challenge which had to be dealt with was that, prior to the reforms, the *Privacy Act* was largely limited to regulating APP entities and didn't account for individuals seeking redress in the courts. Specifically, it focused on governing information privacy (or data protection) which refers to the rules governing the collection and handling of personal information by APP entities (organisations and agencies regulated by the Australian Privacy Principles).[^16] Where individuals believed their data had been mishandled, they were restricted to making a complaint through the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC), with no specific right of action in court.

In order to expand application of the right to privacy to individuals, it was proposed that Schedule 2 be inserted into the *Privacy Act*, to be read separately from the rest of the Act.[^17] This positioned the tort as distinct from the rest of the *Privacy Act*, which is largely focused on regulating the APP entities. Under the reforms, it was proposed that a plaintiff must be an individual. This was a statutory formalisation of the High Court's reasoning in *Lenah Game Meats*, which held that an invasion of privacy is not something a company can claim.[^18]

c)  **Elements of the Tort for Serious Invasions of Privacy**

For an individual (plaintiff) to succeed in an action under the statutory tort for serious invasion of privacy against another person (the defendant), the plaintiff must prove the following elements:

- the defendant invaded the plaintiff's privacy by intruding upon the plaintiff's seclusion and/or misusing information that relates to the plaintiff.

- the plaintiff would have had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the circumstances.

- the invasion of privacy was intentional or reckless.

- the invasion of privacy was serious; and,

- the public interest in the plaintiff's privacy outweighed any other countervailing public interest

> Additionally, there is no need for the plaintiff to show they have suffered any damage as a result of the invasion of privacy.

d)  **Defences to the Tort and Balancing Privacy with Competing Interests**

In the lead up to the release of the *Serious Invasions of Privacy in the Digital Era* report, consultation showed strong support for a statutory tort but also highlighted opposition. One of the key competing views to allowing such a tort was that it could hinder freedom of speech, the media, public health and safety, and national security.[^19] Recommendations therefore flagged a "crucial balancing exercise" against public interest.[^20]

This concern was incorporated into the legislation through the inclusion of a public interest balancing test.[^21] The final release of the Act now directs courts to consider countervailing public interests, such as freedom of expression and the role of the media and journalism, when determining liability. This ensures that the tort cannot be used to suppress matters of legitimate public concern.

> Schedule 2 of the Act also provides other specific defences and exemptions, including:

- Where an invasion of privacy is done under lawful authority, with consent or out of necessity

- Publication-related matters

- Exemptions for agencies, such as intelligence and law enforcement bodies (and people disclosing to such bodies)

e)  **Remedies and Damages**

In addition to the available defences and exemptions, Schedule 2 also empowers courts to award damages or grant other remedies where a serious invasion of privacy is proven.[^22] These remedies include injunctions to restrain further invasions of privacy, declarations and damages. Damages may cover both economic and non-economic loss, including compensation for emotional distress.[^23] The framework also places certain limits on damages. Aggravated damages are excluded, but exemplary or punitive damages may be awarded in exceptional circumstances.[^24]

When assessing damages, courts may also consider factors such as whether the defendant apologised to the plaintiff, corrected a publication, whether the plaintiff received compensation, whether reasonable steps were taken to settle the dispute, and whether the defendant engaged in unreasonable conduct during or after the invasion of privacy.[^25]

f)  **Current Status (to August 2025)**

As of August 2025, the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy is yet to be applied in the courts. However, individuals are now provided greater assurance that their statutory right to privacy will be protected, resolving a long-standing ambiguity in Australian privacy law.
