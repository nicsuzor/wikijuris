### A Case Study: Social Media Minimum Age Legislation and Rules

In December of 2024, the Social Media Minimum Age Bill was passed, and Part 4A of the Online Safety Act was added, which introduced rules requiring social media platforms to 'take reasonable steps to prevent children who have not reached a minimum age from having accounts'. Following enacting of the legislation, the Australian eSafety Commissioner, in partnership with the Australian community, conducted research and consultation on how to implement the legislation, and what guidelines social media platforms should be subject to regarding the reasonable steps required by legislation. Social media platforms will be expected to take the reasonable steps laid out in those guidelines on 10 December 2025, if they have not already done so.

This legislation is a modern-day example of strategies the Australian government is employing in its attempts to regulate the internet and its use. By the law being focused on the social media platforms, instead of their users, it is not only able to be more effectively and efficiently enforced (because there are exponentially less platforms then users), but it also places the onus of compliance on the corporations behind the platforms, instead of the young people or their parents, corporations which have a great financial interest in ensuring that reasonable steps are taken to comply with the legislation to avoid hefty fines and other legal consequences.

Whether this legislation will be effective is yet to be seen. As Barlow expressed in his claim of the unregulatable nature of the internet, there are challenges inherent to its regulation which all bodies attempting to regulate it must attempt to circumvent. As discussed above in this textbook, Barlow's two main arguments in relation to his claim is the de-centralized and ultra-jurisdictional nature of the internet, and the sheer enormity of its reach and quantity of users. The internet is not based anywhere; it does not have one master. There is not one hub at which all content, control, or data is localized, instead there are countless devices connected via networks. Maslow is correct when he says that regulating every contributor to the internet would be a monumental, and likely impossible, feat, and a landmine of legal and jurisdictional issues.

Interestingly, it seems that this new legislation sidesteps those issues neatly, finding a way around many of the challenges and forging a path to possible success in a way which may not have been possible in the past, before social media platforms were as large and powerful as they are today.

By controlling the platforms instead of the users, the legislation not only dramatically decreases the number of identities subject to the legislation, but also creates an environment where it is relatively straightforward to recognize and name the legal identities effected, and pinpoint what jurisdiction is capable of enforcement in relation to those identities.

The legislators are not blind to the possibility of children finding ways around whatever safeguards the social media platforms put in place, and do not seem to be placing an overly heavy burden on the social media platforms to ensure that no children are present on their platforms. The legislation simply requires reasonable steps to be taken. At this point in time (August 2025), the guidelines depicting what those reasonable steps should look like have not yet been released and so exactly how the change will affect users and platforms is unclear.

The Social Media (Minimum Age) legislation illustrates how legislation may be able to avoid the challenges inherent to the regulation of internet, and forge a path towards successful regulation, for better or for worse. Only time will tell how successful this particular attempt at regulation will be, but it signifies the legislator's willingness to take dramatic steps towards regulation.
