**[Copyright Issues in the Generative Artificial Intelligence World]{.underline}**

1.  **[Introduction]{.underline}**

> The increasing use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in creative industries has introduced significant challenges for copyright law. GenAI systems are capable of producing outputs that closely resemble existing works or imitate human creators, often without direct human authorship. These developments raise complex legal questions regarding the ownership and protection of AI-generated content, particularly in contexts where syntenic outputs are indistinguishable from those of real artists. This contribution explores the copyright implications of GenAI through an examination of its use in popular culture and a comparative analysis of authorship under Australian, United Kingdon and United States law.

2.  **[What is Gen-AI]{.underline}**

> GenAI refers to a category of artificial intelligence systems that produce new material, such as images, text or audio, by identifying and replicating patterns in existing data[^1]. These systems, typically trained by large data sets, are capable of generating outputs that appear original but are shaped by the content on which they were trained[^2]. The outputs often involve substantial resemblance to copyrighted works or styles, raising questions of whether they are derivative or infringing of original work.

3.  **[Gen-AI in Popular Culture]{.underline}**

> Traditionally, Artificial Intelligence has been used to help musicians bridge gaps in their skill sets, whether by adding instruments they are unable to play or by generating complex and unconventional sounds which might otherwise take significant time or expertise to create manually[^3]. In more recent years, the rise of voicing cloning, also known as audio deepfakes, has pushed GenAI tools into far more controversial territory. This technology allows for the creation of synthetic voices which can sing or speak in the likeness of real artists, blurring the lines between creative assistance and imitation[^4]. As Zachary Cooper argues, the use of GenAI tools like voice cloning and audio deepfakes challenge the very foundation of copyright law[^5]. When AI is used not just to support but to replicate human expression, it becomes increasingly difficult, if not impossible to determine where human authorship ends, and machine generation begins. Such practices have generated considerable legal uncertainty and have promoted significant opposition from rights holders in the music industry.

4.  **[Case Study -- Heart on my Sleeve]{.underline}**

> A prominent example of these concerns occurred in April 2023 when a song titled '*Heart on my Sleeve*' went viral after it was released on the streaming platform Spotify by an anonymous creator known as \@ghostwriter[^6]. The song appeared to featured vocals from artist Drake and The Weekend, two well know pop stars. However, in reality, \@ghostwriter had used generative AI to voice clone both artists creating synthetic performances without their involvement or consent. The incident sparked widespread debate across the music industry, with Universal Music Group (UMG) condemning the track as infringing content[^7]. Universal Music Group described the release as a breach of both contractual and legal rights, asserting that the unauthorised use of its artists' likenesses, enabled by GenAI, constituted a "*violation of copyright and a broader threat to the integrity of human creative expression*"[^8]. The label warned that such practices challenge the entire economic structure of the music industry and undermine the rights of artists to control and profit from their work[^9].

5.  **[Authorship]{.underline}**

> At the core of the legal uncertainty surrounding generative AI is the question of authorship. Copyright law has historically been structured around the principle that protection subsists in original works created by human authors through the exercise of skill, labour, and intellectual effort[^10]. Generative AI, which produces content without human creativity in the conventional sense, challenges the applicability of this framework[^11]. In jurisdictions such as Australia, the United States and the Unites Kingdom, courts and policy makers have struggled to reconcile the outputs of non-human systems and doctrinal requirements grounded in human agency.

6.  **[Current Australian Law]{.underline}**

> Under Australian copyright law, a fundamental issue posed by generative AI is whether the outputs produced by such systems can attract copyright protection, and if so, by whom that protection vests. The *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) stipulates that copyright subsist only in works that are both original and created by a human author[^12]. This includes categories such as musical composition, lyrics, sound recordings and performances, provided they demonstrate a sufficient degree of originality and are not merely derivative[^13]. Section 32 of the Act provides that copyright subsists in original works where the author is a "qualified person" which is defined as an Australian citizen, permanent resident or protected person under the legislation[^14]. This statutory framework does not accommodate works generated solely by machines. Where an AI system independently produces a musical composition or synthetic performance, there is no legal author for the purposes of the Act[^15]. Consequently, such works may not attract copyright protection and may be treated as being in the public domain, regardless of their commercial or cultural value.

7.  **[Current United States Law]{.underline}**

> In the United States, the U.S. Copyright Office has made it clear that copyright protection is limited to works created by natural persons. In its March 2023 guidance, the Office reiterated that "*the term 'author' in the Copyright Act excludes non-human agents,"* and it has consistently refused to register works generated entirely by AI systems without sufficient human input[^16]. This position was reinforced in *Thaler v Perlmutter* (2023) 1:22-cv-01564-BAH, where a federal district court upheld the Copyright Office's rejection of work created by Dr Stephen Thaler's AI system 'Creativity Machine' on the basis that human authorship is constitutional requirement under the Copyright Clause[^17]. The U.S. Court of Appeal for the D.C Circuit further affirmed this decision in 2025 reasoning that the Copyright Act "*requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being*"[^18]. The emphasis in US law on human intellectual conception effectively excludes autonomous AI-generated works from protection.

8.  **[Current United Kingdom Law]{.underline}**

> Under the *Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988* (CDPA), the United Kingdom occupies a distinctive position in its treatment of AI-generated works[^19]. Section 9(3) provides that for "literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, which is computer-generated, the author shall be taken to be the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken"[^20]. This provision allows for copyright to subsist in computer-generated works where no human author can be identified, attributing authorship to the person who operated or configures the system. However, this attribution is not based on a test of intellectual effort or creativity, instead based off function and procedures[^21]. The provision was introduced at a time where computer-generated content was basic and rules-bases, and its adequacy in the context of large scale generative-AI is increasingly questions[^22]. The threshold for protection remains unclear, and UK courts have yet to interpret this provision in the context of modern AI systems trained in vast datasets and capable of autonomous learning[^23].

9.  **[International Approach]{.underline}**

> All approaches highlight the unresolved tension between traditional concepts of authorships and realities of automated production. The lack of international harmonisation on the issue of AI authorship further complicates the legal landscape. The Berne Convention, which underpins copyright frameworks in over 180 countries, does not define "author", leaving room for national variation[^24]. This absence of consensus has led to divergent approaches and inconsistent outcomes in relation to AI-generated works[^25], undermining legal certainty for creators, users, and rights holders. As generative-AI becomes more sophisticated and widely adopted in creative industries, the limitations of current authorship doctrines are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. There is a growing need for legislative reform to clarify the status of AI-generated works, whether through the introduction of sui generis rights[^26], the expansion of authorship definitions, or the development of new policy which reflects the hybrid nature of human-machine collaboration.

10. **[Conclusion]{.underline}**

> As GenAI tools continue to shape the production of cultural content, existing copyright frameworks face increasing pressure to adapt. While jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom have introduced mechanisms to accommodate computer-generated works, others maintain a strict human authorships requirement that excludes purely AI-generated content from protection. The divergence in international approaches, coupled with the legal uncertainty surrounding imitation, identity and ownership, highlights the urgent need for reform. Clarifying the legal status of AI-generated works will be essential to ensure that copyright law remains relevant and enforceable in an era of machine-generated creativity.
