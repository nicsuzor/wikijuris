[AI Voice Agents and Characters]{.underline}

AI voice agents use natural language processing and generative AI to simulate conversation with humans. In practice an agent will listen, understand and respond with a realistic voice in a

conversational way -- as a human would. Depending on what is has been programmed to do, the voice agent can perform tasks, answer questions, book appointments and transfer calls to human employees.

A more complex version of these AI voice agents are AI characters, which are specifically designed to simulate a personality or character. These characters can be fictional or mimic real people and are often used for entertainment and companionship.

Unlike Siri or Alexa which are task-based agents, AI characters can display emotions, remember conversations and adapt over time maintaining long term connections with the people who engage them.

[Social Context and Potential Harms]{.underline}

The main concern with these characters is that overtime it may be hard for users (especially children and vulnerable people) to distinguish between programming and reality. The 24/7 availability of the characters, custom programming and empathetic responses creates a risk of emotional dependency and distorted understandings of intimacy. Unlike in human relationships where interactions are

varied (both positive and negative), AI characters will always respond in emotionally consistent and affirming ways.

Similarly, the lack of built-in safeguards or age restrictions means that children can have

inappropriate or harmful conversations with AI characters. Concerningly, AI characters have been reported to normalise and even celebrate user-initiated erotic role play, self-harm disclosures and violent speech.

Importantly, AI characters can impair the emotional and social development of dependent users and continuous exposure to permissive AI companions can erode one\'s empathy and understandings of consent.

[Australian Legal Framework]{.underline}

From a regulation standpoint Australia treats AI agents/ characters as online content, which is primarily regulated by the *Online Safety Act 2021(Cth).*

Under the Online Safety Act the eSafety Commissioner is empowered to regulate harms linked to cyberbullying, adult cyber abuse, image-based abuse and illegal/restricted content. Moreover, the Online Safety (Basic Online Safety Expectations) Determination 2022, made under section 45 of the

Act reduces the risk of misusing AI by requiring internet service providers to take reasonable steps to ensure AI is delivered safety.

The Privacy Act 1998 (Cth) also plays a role in regulating the collection, use and disclosure of sensitive information online.

To date Australia does not have a binding legislative framework to regulate AI or AI characters specifically. In its place, the government has instituted other soft law measures.

Although lacking enforcement, Australia's Artificial Ethics Principles ensure AI systems:

- benefit individuals, society and the environment

- respect human rights, diversity, and the autonomy of individuals

- are inclusive, accessible and do not involve or result in unfair discrimination against individuals, communities or groups

- uphold privacy rights and data protection and ensure the security of data

- are transparent so people can understand when they are being impacted by AI. The ethics principles encourage:

<!-- -->

- Accountability - people making the AI characters are accountable for the outcomes of the AI systems; and

- Contestability - When an AI system significantly impacts a person, community, group or environment, there must be a forum for people to challenge the use or outcomes of the AI characters.

[Legal and Ethical Issues]{.underline}

Given the nuance and constant developments in AI, lack of a specific, enforceable framework raises significant legal and ethical questions that are not easily answered.

For example:

- If an AI companion solicits violence or sexualised content that results in harm, who is responsible? Is it the developer who made it, the hosting platform who made it available or the user who promoted it?

- When AI characters mimic real people without consent, does this amount to a breach or privacy or intellectual property?

- If someone is harmed psychologically or socially by an AI character, what redress is available?

- How can the government stop vulnerable users from developing dependencies on AI characters without unfairly restricting their use compared to less susceptible populations?

- Will AI characters that have been programmed as educational or therapeutic be restricted in the same way as "harmful" AI characters?

In an attempt to clarify some of these questions, the EU and China have instead adopted a legislative approach.

[International Approaches]{.underline}

### In Europe

> *The European Artificial Intelligence Act (2024)*

- Categorises AI systems by risk - ranging from minimal risk to unacceptable risk.

- Prohibits use of AI in the unacceptable risk category -- including but not limited to manipulative AI and social scoring AI that is used by governments.

- AI characters are considered high risk and have strict regulatory requirements for use.

- Requires voice agents and characters to identify themselves as AI before engaging with users.

- Places strict obligations on developers to ensure safety of AI systems.

- Mandates transparency, impersonation and content disclosure.

### In China

> *The Administrative Provisions on Deep Synthesis in internet-Based Information Services (2023)*

- Requires explicit labelling of AI generated voices and prohibit cloning without consent.

- Requires service providers to verify users\' real identities before offering AI abilities to them.

- Prohibits AI content from being used for harmful or illegal content.

- Restricts services from editing a user\'s biometric features (including face and voice) without their consent.

- Requires platforms to monitor network logs, respond to harmful content and have a forum for complaints.

Both international frameworks preference binding obligations on developers and service providers. The framework ensures transparency, safety and accountability in AI by design and is contrasted with Australia's soft-law approach that offers principles in place of regulation. Comparing the approaches highlights the large regulatory gap that remains for AI characters and the unique challenges they pose to the regulation of online content.
