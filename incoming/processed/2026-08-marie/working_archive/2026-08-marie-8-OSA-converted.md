**[Office of the eSafety Commissioner]{.underline}**

In Australia, eSafety first arose with the *Enhancing Online Safety for Children Act 2015 (Cth),* which provided for the appointment of an independent Office of the Children's eSafety Commissioner.[^1] Under this act, the Commissioner was provided with the power to do all things necessary to uphold its functions, those largely relating to the promotion of safety for Australian children online.[^2] A primary function of the Commissioner under this act was the administration of a tiered complaints system that allowed for rapid removal or removal requests of cyber-bullying material targeted at Australian children.[^3]

In 2017 the *Enhancing Online Safety for Children Act* was amended, renaming the act and expanding the role of the eSafety Commissioner to protect all Australians from abuse online.[^4] Further changes were then made to the newly dubbed *Enhancing Online Safety Act 2015 (Cth)* between 2017 and 2021, providing the eSafety Commissioner with greater powers and more expansive functions in protecting Australians online.[^5] In particular, eSafety had the power by 2021 to not only administer its cyber-bullying complaints system, but also monitor content and address issues around the non-consensual sharing of intimate images ('revenge porn') online.[^6]

Among other key legislation, the *Enhancing Online Safety Act* was finally replaced by the *Online Safety Act 2021 (Cth) ('OSA'),* which commenced in 2022 and has streamlined online regulation as we know it today.[^7] The OSA further expands the eSafety Commissioner's powers in administering its functions, with a newfound focus on issues including content removal, basic safety expectations for online services, abhorrent violent material and recently, social media age restrictions.[^8]

1.  **Online Safety Act 2021: Statutory Review**

Under *Section 239A OSA*, the Minister of Communications must ensure that within three years of commencement, a review is undertaken with subsequent reporting provided on the operative effectiveness of the enactment.[^9]

This review was announced early by the Government on 22 November 2023, brought forward a year to ensure that Australia's online safety laws were keeping pace with the evolving digital environment.[^10] In April 2024, an issues paper was released to assist the public in making informed contributions to this statutory review process, with a particular focus on the following five key topics:[^11]

1.  *Australia's regulatory approach to online services, systems and processes.*

2.  *Protecting those who have experienced or encountered online harms.*

3.  *Penalties, investigation, and information gathering powers.*

4.  *International approaches to address online harms.*

5.  *Regulating the online environment, technology, and environmental changes.*

On 31 October 2024, the independent review of the OSA was complete with the final report being provided to the Australian Government for consideration towards reform.[^12]

a.  ***Statutory Review Outcome***

In February 2025, the Australian Parliament tabled the final report on the statutory review of the OSA.[^13] In examining the operative effectiveness of the OSA and considering whether additional safeguards were needed to combat issues online, the report provided 67 recommendations to enhance and future-proof Australia's online safety.[^14]

Some key recommendations arising from this report can be summarised as follows:

a.  *Establishing a Digital Duty of Care*

The primary recommendation arising from this report revolves around implementing a digital duty of care that will shift the onus of preventing foreseeable harm online from end-users to service providers.[^15] This duty would involve service providers undertaking risk assessment with due diligence in providing their services, with an increased focus on risk prevention through systems design, mitigation strategies and enhanced transparency reporting.[^16]

In effect, the report argues that shifting onus away from disadvantaged individuals to capable service providers to prevent online harms will facilitate a more proactive approach to online safety with greater impact than Australia's current reactive model.[^17] Furthermore, the report claims that such duty will be beneficial for all those involved as it will simplify compliance and costs in adhering to online safety regulation while enhancing protection for Australians online.[^18]

Based on this report, the Albanese Government have already committed to legislating a digital duty of care in a bid to future-proof the OSA and keep Australians safe from online harm.[^19] For more information on this duty, see the recently proposed *Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care Bill) 2024 (Cth).*

b.  *Enhancing eSafety Powers and Functions*

Many additional recommendations provided by the report revolve around enhancing eSafety power in protecting Australians online, with a focus on strengthening investigatory functions to better facilitate enforcement against online harm.[^20] Furthermore, the report envisions greater penalties for non-compliance with online regulation by service providers to better support eSafety in taking a more proactive approach to online harm prevention.[^21]

In respect to the OSA's existing content take-down scheme, the report recommends general improvements to accessibility and versatility to assist users seeking to address online harm in a contemporary digital environment.[^22] Finally, in terms of the proposed digital duty of care, the report recommends eSafety be given the power to create mandatory compliance codes for service providers alongside existing industry-led processes to better ensure conformity with online regulation.[^23]

c.  *Moving eSafety to a 'Commission Model'*

Recognising the vast and growing functions of the eSafety Commissioner, the report recommends that the regulatory body move to a commission-based model with collective decision-making powers to strengthen the governance of Australia's online safety.[^24] Ideally, this *Online Safety Commission* would be an accountable, independent body with the necessary skills and experience to support the vast functions of eSafety in an advancing digital environment.[^25]

For now, the report suggests moving to a model comprising a chairperson and deputy chairperson alongside the eSafety Commissioner to enhance regulatory decision-making and better distribute the Commissioner's growing workload.[^26]

d.  *Simplifying Australian Online Regulation*

To facilitate stronger compliance with online regulation, the report recommends simplification under the OSA to better classify key actors in the digital services industry and to provide definitions around contemporary harms affecting Australian's online.[^27] Further to this, the report suggests decoupling the OSA from the National Classification Scheme to better reflect a regulatory division between harmful content made professionally and that which is user-generated.[^28]

In terms of classifying key actors in the digital service industry, the report suggests that change is needed here to avoid confusion and uncertainty in applying online regulation.[^29] In particular, the report recommends a shift away from classifications focused on services provided to end-users and towards a role-based system under the following categories:[^30]

- *Online platforms*

- *Online Search and App Distribution Services*

- *Online Infrastructure Services*

- *Equipment and Operating System Services*

Finally, in respect to decoupling the OSA from the National Classification Scheme, the report argues that such division is necessary as existing content standards are not suitable for responding to illegal or harmful user-generated content.[^31] In effect, the report envisions greater boundaries between the two instruments and updated classifications for online content to simplify regulation while enhancing response measures to increasingly harmful content online.[^32]
