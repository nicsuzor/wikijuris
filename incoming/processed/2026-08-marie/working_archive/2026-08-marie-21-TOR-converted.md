[**9(a)(i)** **The TOR Network (i.e. the 'Onion Router'):**]{.underline}

One popularised cryptography tool used internationally includes the TOR network (also known as the 'onion router') ('TOR'). This network works by concealing a user's IP address through encryption and connections to anonymous private networks. Each message is encrypted by an onion router, which is then decrypted by the next onion router as it passes through the network. Each of these onion routers are known as a TOR node; there are four types:

(1) TOR exit node -- traffic passes through this node before exiting into the internet

(2) TOR guard node -- point of entry into the TOR network

(3) TOR middle node -- located between the guard and exit node. A message may travel through multiple middle nodes before exiting.

(4) TOR bridge node -- specific type of guard node.

Using a combination of TOR nodes to relay a message, it is nearly impossible to decrypt the message, as not one router can see both the original source and the destination address.[^1]

The creation of TOR began in the mid 90s by the US Navy with the intent to protect US intelligence communications. Initially, TOR was only to be used by the military and law enforcements. However, it was soon realised that outsiders, such as malicious actors, could easily identify any TOR connections with a law official. In September 2002, TOR was thus expanded and released to the public for all to use.[^2] TOR is currently maintained by the non-profit 'TOR Project' and receives funding from a range of institutions. TOR can be used for anonymous browsing, as well as the hosting of anonymous information exchanges.[^3]

TOR AND THE *PRIVACY ACT 1988* (CTH)

As one of the most efficient and popular cryptography tools, TOR enhances the privacy and anonymity of the user.[^4] As described above in s 4(c) of this textbook, the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) ('*Privacy Act*') encompasses a range of privacy principles ('APP') that work to regulate internet usage and are overseen by the Office of Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC).[^5] Cryptography, such as TOR, is considered to fall under principle 2 of the APP, which explicitly state that individual users should have the option to remain anonymous.[^6]

Principle 11 indicates that an entity is required to take reasonable steps to destroy or de-identify personal information.[^7] However, this conflicts with the *Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act* 1979, which mandates that internet entities must retain metadata for seven years.[^8] This law has been challenged by many through the use of cryptology, such as TOR. That is, many individuals use TOR to keep their personal data hidden, protecting their information from being collected and stored. Moreover, the *Telecommunications and Other Legislation Amendment (Assistance and Access) Act 2018* (Cth) provides that the government can use decryption tools to identify a user, giving officials power to issue notices to gain access to the data an internet provider stores.[^9] This amendment has been met with controversy.[^10] Hence, it has been argued that there needs to be balance between upholding one's freedom of privacy, but also regulation of malicious actors exploiting cryptography tools for illicit purposes.[^11]

As of 2025, there is no legislation prohibiting the use of cryptography tools such as TOR. Countries such as China, Russia and North Korea have put measures in place to block TOR and penalise TOR users, due to its access to unrestricted material and anonymity.[^12]

TOR AND THE DARK WEB:

One of the popularised methods of accessing the dark web is through TOR as it provides a more secure and private access. It also provides a range of hidden services through websites that are only applicable by using a TOR browser, with these websites ending in '.onion'. TOR has earned a reputation of being used only for illegal purposes.[^13]

However, those accessing the dark web are not always for malicious intentions. Many users access the dark web from repressive countries to avoid censorship, such as journalists; whistleblowers and political activists access the dark web to report abuse anonymously from dangerous locations; and law enforcements access the dark web to monitor illegal activity.[^14] Using TOR to access the dark web for these purposes provides a safe pathway to report abuse; for example, Syrians have been known to access TOR to report corruption and abuse committed by the Assad regime -- as TOR guarantees their identities will remain hidden.[^15]

Unfortunately, TOR allows malicious actors to access the dark web for illegal purposes. These users have used the dark web to traffic narcotics and weapons, distribute child abuse material, and terrorism purposes. The following recent cases exemplify the dark side of the dark web, and how TOR can assist individuals in accessing illicit material:

1.  In *Aitken (A Pseudonym) v The King* \[2025\], the applicant was charged with black mail and threatening a high school. The Court gave permission for the Police to use Digital Forensics (AFP) and Digital Evidence Section (SAPOL) to decrypt the applicant's VPN and Onion Router to access his online history.[^16]

2.  In *Director of Public Prosecutions (Cth) v XYZ (A Pseudonym)* \[2024\], the applicant used TOR to access the dark web to download and possess child abuse material; investigators successfully decrypted the applicant's online history.[^17]

3.  In *Attorney-General for the State of Queensland v GFA* \[2025\], the applicant accessed child abuse material; the Court ordered that the applicant must not keep his identity hidden online, and thus ordered the applicant not to download or use any cryptography tools, such as TOR.[^18]

> The Onion Router can provide both advantages and disadvantages when it comes to accessing the internet, dependent on the users intention. It protects the identity of a user and protects their data and personal information. The results of a [recent study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10439862231157553) on TOR users browser habits have suggested that the vast majority of users utilise this tool for a desire for privacy.[^19] However, malicious actors may abuse the privacy of the onion router and use it to conceal their identity to engage in illegal activity.
