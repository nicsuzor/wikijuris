> **The *Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act***

# 2024

> Today's social media environment has rapidly become extremely personalised, accessible and embedded into various areas of our daily lives. Unlike ten years ago, social media algorithms are now complex and sophisBcated, with extensive research highlighBng concerns about eﬀects of social media usage on various areas of wellbeing and health - especially for children.
>
> The *Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act* 2024 (SMMA) was passed in November 2024, introducing a minimum social media age -- the ﬁrst law of its kind globally. This means that from 10 December 2025, age-restricted social media platforms must take 'reasonable steps' to prevent Australian children under the age of 16 from creaBng or keeping an account on their platform.

## Aims of the SMMA Act

> The legislation is guided by a proactive child-protection regulatory model. This model aims to protect young Australians from exposure to the dangers and risks associated with social media and other online content. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese stated the ban aims to ensure 'children have a childhood and parents have peace of mind.'^1^ It is a response to the growing contemporary concern towards the dangers brought by 'co-mingled platforms' that were designed for adults but are now occupied by children.^2^
>
> The purpose of the minimum age restriction is to protect, rather than punish, young users. Responsibility is placed on social media companies that run these platforms, away from the parents or children aged under 16 that use them. Neither underage children accessing age-restricted social media platforms or their parents will be penalised. According to the e-Safety Commissioner, the minimum social media regulations are a 'delay' rather than an 'outright ban', putting accountability where it belongs - on platforms themselves.^3^

## Impact of Social Media on Young People

### Key Statistics

> It is useful to explore the current impact of social media on young Australians, and the data that informed the minimum age restricBon. [Research]{.underline} completed by e-Safety in 2024 revealed that 96% of children aged 10-15 reported using social media at some point in their lives. Over 71% had encountered content associated with harm and more than half of children aged 13-15 had experienced cyberbullying. One in seven children reported experiencing online grooming-like behaviour from adults or other children at least four years older.^4^
>
> A separate [study]{.underline} by the Australian InsBtute of Health and Welfare found young Australians experience higher levels of psychological distress compared to older groups, with young females experiencing the highest levels of psychological distress.^5^

## What Social Media Platforms Are Impacted?

> These age restrictions will apply to social media platforms that meet the following three conditions:

1.  The only, or a signiﬁcant, purpose of the service is to allow online social interacBon between two or more end-users;

2.  The service allows users to link to, or interact with, some or all of the other users; and

3.  The service allows users to post material on the platform.^6^

> Platforms that will likely meet these condiBons include Snapchat, Facebook, TikTok, Instagram and X (formerly Twitter).

## Age Assurance Technologies

> To implement the age restrictions, platforms will need to use age assurance technologies in an eﬀort to take 'reasonable steps' in meeting their new obligations.
>
> 'Age Assurance' refers to the various methods used to determine a person's age. These methods include a high degree of certainty while others simply provide an esBmate or range for a person's age.^10^ For example, Instagram (Meta) users are now required to provide their date of birth when creaBng an account, with further veriﬁcation needed if user's change their age from under to over 18 after creating their account. One way of verifying this is by uploading a selﬁe video to be analysed by facial age esBmaBon technology.^11^
>
> In 2024-25, the Australian Government funded an Age Assurance Technology Trial to test the technologies that could carry out the new minimum age ban. The trial reported that age assurance is possible in Australia, while also highlighBng shortcomings and risks with the technologies tested. The ﬁndings emphasised that there is no one-size-ﬁts-all solution. Technologies will likely require multiple fall-back options where age estimation technology fails, such as ID checks.^12^ Interestingly, the report found age estimation was unable to provide precise esBmates for 'non-white faces' and for girls compared to boys. While systems generally align with cybersecurity best practice, caution is required.

## Criticisms of the SMMA Act

a.  **Protection or Exclusion?**

> Regulation of online content unvaryingly raises tension between the protection of users from harm and the preservation of freedom of expression.12 Australia is the ﬁrst jurisdiction to impose such a strict standard for content regulation.13 Yet despite the potential beneﬁts of minimising children's access to social media, there is considerable debate as to whether this type of regulation is disproportionate to readily protecting children at large.14 Rather than regulating harmful content directly, the Amendment adopts a blanket prohibition that risks excluding children from valuable sources of support.15

(a) *Human Rights Concerns*

> The Australian Human Rights Commission ("*AHRC*") has expressed serious reservations about the proposed social media ban for those under 16 and recommended that the Amendment not pass in its current form.16
>
> Under human rights law, limitations on rights must be necessary and proportionate to pursue a legitimate aim.17 To the AHRC, this means using the least restrictive option available to achieve the intended purpose.18 The AHRC accepted that protecting children from the negative impacts of social media is consistent with the aim in Art. 17 of the *UN Committee on the Rights of the Ch*ild ("*UNCRC*"). That being, to develop appropriate guidelines to protect children from information and material that harms their well-being. However, it is the AHRC's strong belief that the social media ban is also likely to have negative human rights impacts.19 In it's submission to Senate, the AHRC argued that social media provides opportunities for young people to access inclusion and "*critical, potentially life-saving information*", and hence reasoned that this amendment is disproportionate to the overarching aim.20
>
> Further, the AHRC has identiﬁed that this 'blanket ban' on social media undermines the UNCRC recognition of children's rights to freedom of expression (Art. 13) and access to information from diverse media (Art. 17).21 In contemplating these rights, the United Nations Committee have emphasised that content moderation and controls should not restrict children's access to information in the digital environment, but should instead be used to prevent the ﬂow of harmful material to them.22 Australia's restrictive approach does not appear to align with this standard.
>
> From a human rights perspective, if there are less restrictive options available to protect children from harm without undermining children's rights to inclusion and expression, they should be preferred over a blanket ban.23
