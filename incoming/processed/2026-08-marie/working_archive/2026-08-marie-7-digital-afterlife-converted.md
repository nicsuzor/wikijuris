**D social media and Death**

With the increased usage of social media in the last two decades there has been an unforeseen issue when digital content outlives the content creator.[^1] The number of older generations (Gen X and Baby Boomers) daily accessing and using social media has increased with the gap narrowing with younger generations to 27%. On average 91% of Baby Boomers and older are present on multiple social media sites for up to 20 hours per week increasing their digital footprint when they die.

**Aspects of Digital Life**

The Australian eSafety Commissioner defines the term digital content into two separate definitions: digital assets and digital presence.[^2]

**Digital Assets**

The category of digital assets contains any online purchase a person has made in their life including eBooks, domain names, passwords, accounts etc. With online purchases the right to pass on the item after death is impacted by the companies' terms of use which place limitations on digital ownership and transferability.

The increase in digital ownership over physical has altered the way that legal documents, such as wills, are constructed.[^3] Only 33% of the general population understood and comprehended the different requirements that digital assets have compared to physical post-mortem. This is compounded when the majority of the population wait until after the age of 50 to start considering the creation of a will and what items to put inside. The delay in creating a will and the disregard of digital assets increases the legal interactions between family members and the online providers.

**Digital Prescence**

The category of digital presence is the online persona created through social media posts with other people that you may not know in person.[^4] This persona is carefully curated for many different reasons from communicating with friends online to self-promotion at work and can be easily damaged.[^5] The main drawback of having a digital presence is the ease of which friends and relatives can intentionally and accidentally increase a digital footprint with reposts and the uploading of new photos.[^6]

Currently social media platforms have responded in different ways regarding the accounts of deceased users with either memorialisation (Facebook) or deletion. Memorialisation restricts the use of the accounts content to current friends and family and prevents anyone from searching for the account. This prevents the memorialised content from being accessed for marketing reasons but blocks any associate of the deceased from accessing and befriending the page after death.

**Digital Grieving**

Current developments in artificial intelligence have allowed for a deceased person to be recreated from movies and photos to communicate with their loved ones.[^7] Developed in 2022 the creation of a digital doppelgänger has been a common feature in Chinese funerals to assist with the grieving process. Originally the service was limited but has expanded allowing families to choose what package they can afford giving a more comprehensive digital copy that behaves and sounds like the deceased person. The unforeseen nature of some deaths create ethical issues surrounding the consent of the deceased person to access and use their digital presence in this manner.

**Regulation**

Australian legislation is currently lacking regarding the use and ownership of digital content and the authority to act.[^8] The *Online Safety Act* 2021(Cth) provides the bulk of legislation regarding social media and while it is currently being updated[^9] it is quiet on any information regarding the upkeep of accounts after death. The eSafety Commissioner provides tips on dealing with social media companies and their terms of services on how to decommission or memorialise an account after the original owner has died.[^10]

The regulation of deceased accounts is left to the social media companies and requires the nominated overseer to be proficient in following the necessary steps to protect the account data. Many social media companies restrict access to accounts, both in use and deceased, under the guise of privacy and their agreed to terms of service. The restricted access means that it is more difficult for a deceased account to be deleted or memorialised allowing access to the person's digital content.

Unlike the US Australia does not have and right to privacy laws that prevent the unauthorised use of a person's image. Australians are required to use either defamation law[^11] or consumer law[^12] as a work around depending on various requirements. Defamation law allows for the removal and restitution if the image damage the reputation of the individual[^13] and consumer laws prevent an image from being misleading or deceptive.[^14]
