## What is Technology-facilitated abuse?

Technology-facilitated abuse (TFA) refers to the use of technology to harm or abuse someone. TFA may be used as a form of domestic violence (DV), family violence (FV) or sexual violence by a perpetrator. Such behaviour may include harassing or threatening someone online, including sharing or threatening to share intimate images, known as image-based sexual abuse (IBSA)^1^. Additionally, TFA may include cyberstalking, controlling online communication and/or restricting an individual\'s access to online accounts or technology^2^. Technology-based coercive control can also be a form of DV or FV whereby a perpetrator seeks to control the victim through tactics such as manipulation, pressure and/or fear^3^. This may be seen through denying an individual access to their online social support, tracking, monitoring, or gaslighting and isolating an individual^4^. According to a survey of family and domestic violence practitioners, 99.3% of practitioners had clients who had experienced TFA^5^. Such statistics highlight the prevalence of TFA and the risk technology poses when used as a harmful tool of DV and FV. TFA has increased over recent years, especially following the increased reliance on online communication following COVID-19^6^. TFA is included within the *National Plan to End Violence Against Women and Children* 2022-2032 as a key concern within FV and DV. TFA therefore requires serious consideration in order to prevent the continuation of technology being used as a tool to harm individuals in a domestic, family or sexual violence context. While technology has many benefits, the risks associated with it and the reality of the way in which it can be used as a form of abuse is important to consider and ensure appropriate action is taken.

## Case Study

> In 2023, a 21 year old woman was murdered by a man she had been in a short relationship with. The perpetrator used a social media app, Snapchat, to track her location. The devastating loss of Lilie James highlights the risks associated with using social media apps which allow tracking and monitoring of others. Lilie's perpetrator had a history of coercive control, such as his anger towards his former partner when she would not share her iphone location with him^7^. The perpetrator became angry with Lilie following her rejection of him and grew jealous when he viewed her location on Snap Map, becoming aware that she was at a party with an ex, before murdering her at a school in Sydney where they both worked^8^. The perpetrator had also used IBSA as a form of control by showing his friends intimate images of Lilie. In the inquest into Lilie James' death, her perpetrator's use of social media was described as TFA and that this form of monitoring is a well recognised form of digital coercive control^9^. DV, including TFA has been described as an 'epidemic against women', highlighting the prevalence and seriousness of this issue, in addition to the gendered nature of DV and FV^10^. Accordingly, women are found to experience TFA at disproportionately higher rates than men^11^.
>
> ESafety Commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, says that tracking another person can be more about control than 'care'^12^. This echoes the insidious way tracking apps can be used and highlights the need to think critically about the use of such technology while taking action to prevent harm through TFA. According to the Commissioner, TFA is increasingly common, especially for younger people as 1 in 4 individuals between 10 to 17 have experienced some form of tracking and harassment^13^. The loss of Lilie James is a heartbreaking reminder of the dangers and risks associated with tracking apps and the way technology can be used as a form of DV and/or FV.

## Role of the eSafety Commissioner

The eSafety Commissioner plays an important role in improving online safety. Reports of serious harmful content such as adult cyber abuse and image based abuse are able to be made to the eSafety Commissioner^16^. The eSafety Commissioner is also able to facilitate online safety plans for individuals at risk or who have experienced TFA^17^. As women are disproportionately victims of TFA the Commissioner provides a program specifically for the protection of women from TFA. The eSafety Commissioner has the power to compel removal of harmful online content within 24 hours, expose non-complying services, impose penalties and reveal the identities of perpetrators^18^.

*Criticisms & Recommendations*

While the eSafety Commissioner plays an important role in addressing TFA, the Commissioner's efforts have been criticised and recommendations for improvement have been suggested. There is no doubt the increasing prevalence of TFA enhances the challenges for the Commissioner to meet the demand in adequately protecting public safety, however, as technology concerns increase, so too should the efforts by the eSafety Commissioner^19^. While the eSafety Commissioner plays an essential role in the removal of harmful online content, the Commissioner has been criticised for not using its 'stronger statutory powers', failing to do more to deter and prevent TFA and failing to create wider systemic change^20^. Solely removing harmful online content has been regarded as 'superficial and ineffective' as it fails to provide adequate support and assistance to TFA victims^21^. Further, according to ANROWS, frontline workers do not always take TFA seriously, reinforcing the need for the eSafety Commissioner to do more in this area^22^. Calls for the eSafety Commissioner to provide more meaningful assistance have therefore been made^23^. Further, increased training, education and resources regarding TFA have been recommended, especially for younger people to better understand TFA and digital coercion as well as reshaping norms regarding digital privacy and consent within relationships^24^.
