# **11d) Protecting Children on Video-Sharing Platforms: Content Regulation and the Law**

This contribution builds upon the regulation of children's exposure on platforms such as TikTok and YouTube, examining how Australia's regulation of harmful online content can heavily impact the protection of younger individuals. It fills a gap in the exploration of 'sharenting', parents posting images and videos of their children online, and the risks this creates for the privacy, exploitation, and algorithmic amplification of children.

## **The Dangers for Children Online**

Social media platforms such as TikTok have transformed the way many families share their lives online. Parents increasingly post videos of their children engaging in everyday activities to document memories and milestones, create entertaining content, or even build their audiences and platforms of viewers. This practice, widely referred to as "sharenting," can result in serious concerns for children's privacy and safety. Innocuous content can quickly be downloaded, shared, or even manipulated, exposing children to risks of exploitation. Reports have also highlighted how TikTok's recommendations and algorithms can amplify this exposure, inadvertently and unknowingly directing predators towards this specific material.

*Australia's Online Safety Act* 2021 (Cth) (OSA) provides one of the most in depth guidelines for addressing online harms, however, it tends to focus mostly on dealing with harmful material after it has been uploaded. The dangers associated with sharenting on TikTok are not considered in depth within the framework of Australia's current regulatory schemes.

### **The Dangers of Parental Posting**

Unlike other concepts covered in this textbook, such as cyberbullying, adult cyber abuse, image-based abuse and so on, the risks of parental posting usually arise from good intentions and actions that unintentionally compromise children's welfare. Some of the obvious and potential dangers of sharenting include:

> **a)** **Lack of** **Consent and Autonomy:** Children cannot provide informed consent to the permanent digital footprint created by their parents' posts. These images and videos have the ability to impact their future and personal life, such as their mental health.
>
> **b)** **Exploitation and Grooming Risks:** Innocent family content can be reused and shared in exploitative ways. TikTok's features, such as "duets" and "stitches", allow strangers to easily redistribute child-focused videos, raising the risk of predatory attention.
>
> **c) Commercialisation:** Parents often monetise their children's online presence through 'influencer' culture (the practice of individuals building large online followings and monetising their personal lives through sponsored content and partnerships). While some jurisdictions (e.g. France) have introduced laws to protect child influencers' earnings and privacy, Australia lacks a comparable framework, leaving children vulnerable to financial exploitation.
>
> **d) Algorithmic Amplification:** TikTok's recommendation system increases virality. Even without bad intentions, a parent's video of their child can be algorithmically distributed to audiences numbering in the millions.

## **11e) Case Study: Wren Eleanor and the Ethics of "Sharenting"**

An example of the risks associated with parental posting is the case of Wren Eleanor, a three-year-old American child whose TikTok account (@wreneleanor), managed by her mother, has gained over 17 million followers over the years. The account gained attention in 2022 when videos of Wren playing with a tampon and taking a bath wearing a bathing suit were uploaded. Although initially the account and content seemed harmless, the videos of Wren were being saved and downloaded tens of thousands of times and started to attract disturbing comments from adult users, such as "there is no way that baby doesn't have a boyfriend" or "ZAYM, SHES 3?".

Some concerned TikTok users started to argue that Wren's mother was exploiting her daughter for financial gain, with estimates suggesting she earned nearly 15,000 American dollars per month from the account. [Jasmine Hood Miller](https://abcnews.go.com/GMA/Family/wren-eleanor-tiktok-trend-sees-parents-removing-photos/story?id=87486106.), director of community content and engagement for Common Sense Media, has warned parents that every photo or video posted online creates a lasting record, stating, *"That's basically an electronic paper trail that doesn't go away for your kids ... so it's something to really, really think about even as early as when you have a newborn and you're so excited and you want to share."*[^1] She stresses that while posting has become "normalised" in today's world, parents may underestimate the long-term risks to their child's privacy and safety.

**Australian and International Regulatory Approach**

The OSA adopts a co-regulatory model, where industry bodies draft codes that are subject to approval by the eSafety Commissioner. In relation to child safety, three key actions are the most relevant: Removal Notices (ss 109--111), Basic Online Safety Expectations (BOSE) Determination 2022, Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024. While actions such as deleting exploitative content, requiring platforms to take "reasonable steps," and preventing children under 16 from holding their own accounts are important steps, they do not address the core problem of parents unknowingly putting their children at risk. We can see from eSafety Commissioner v X Corp \[2024\] FCA 499 that the courts interpret "reasonable steps" narrowly, with geo-blocking deemed sufficient even when harmful content remains globally accessible. It is fair to assume that a similar jurisdictional barrier would apply to the exploitation and redistribution of child based TikTok content, as regulators cannot guarantee the worldwide removal.

In the United Kingdom, the Online Safety Act 2023 has created new offences such as "cyberflashing" and "encouraging self-harm," to show a legislative intent to address digital harms at their source. Similarly, France has taken the step of regulating child influencers. Laws introduced in 2020 require parental income from child social media stars to be set aside in trust.

**Conclusion**

The regulation of parental posting on TikTok exposes two key gaps in Australia's current legal regulatory framework. First, there is no direct regulation of "sharenting". The OSA and other related statutes provide no restrictions on parents publishing their children's images, regardless of the long-term consequences for privacy, autonomy or exploitation. Second, in this specific field, Australia's privacy and data protection laws fall behind some international approaches.
