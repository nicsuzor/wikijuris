**[When Trolling is Beyond Satire and Parody]{.underline}**

Within Australian Internet Law, freedom of speech offers individuals various benefits in their everyday life, including self-expression and empowerment. However, with this inherent right, an abuse of power is always possible, even causing lasting and permanent harm to victims. In an ever-changing technology-based society, online trolling is an emerging issue, with statistics revealing a more alarming reality than what is often believed.[^1] Pursuant to the *Online Safety Act,* it must be recognised that the law must be aware of content regulation; however, does this act protect those vulnerable, and what is inflicted on offenders as a punishment?[^2]

**[Trolling Statistics in Australia]{.underline}**

Trolling, which is defined as an act of posting deliberately offensive or provocative messages online, remains a grey area within Australian law.[^3] A 2023 survey reveals that more than one in three Australians have experienced online trolling, with 44% having at least one negative online experience, and 21% having reported the online harassment.[^4] To summarise, 53% of young Australians have been cyberbullied.[^5] To counter trolling, 54% of Australians aged 12-17 blocked offenders in 2020, which is an increase from 43% in 2017.[^6] Notably, the percentage of young people reaching out to family or friends about trolling dropped from 64% in 2017 to 43% in 2020. This suggests that online trolling has become more normalised, making victims less likely to seek help/report.[^7] Unaddressed, this normalisation can lead to lasting physical and psychological harm.[^8]

Whilst Australia is signatory to the *International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights*[^9]*,* which has been ratified into the *OSA*[^10] and the *Criminal Code Act*[^11], the law is seemingly limited and showcases a disconnection between legislation and technology. Though the *OSA* expands the eSafety Commissioner's powers in dealing with offensive online material, the act remains lacking in providing the public with the exact dangers of trolling through an educational learning system.[^12] Truly, it's acknowledged that it's the users themselves who contribute to trolling and the platforms themselves that do not effectively enforce policies against abusive behaviour and harassment.[^13] With statistics on trolling cumulating, this creates complications for legislation to enforce policy efficiently if no further action is amended.

**[Case Study: Trolling the Trolls -- How Indy Clinton Got the Last Laugh]{.underline}**

In June 2025, Australian influencer Indy Clinton hired a private investigator (PI) to expose the identities of those anonymous users who were harassing and trolling on her social media platforms.[^14] Clinton explained that she came to this decision after 'being trolled mercilessly' for years, announcing this news to her followers via TikTok with a 64-page report disclosing her trollers' identities.[^15] PI Amy Elliot details how Clinton's PI was able to defeat trolls, stating that if individuals want to troll, then they must be aware of the potential consequences, such as a potential claim for defamation.[^16] Elliot clarified that PIs with a police license can legally follow individuals, therefore, not constituting harassment or stalking, and warned trolls about potential legal consequences like defamation claims

Upon investigation, Clinton revealed how many of these trolling accounts were run by mothers, expressing that '\... the damage is done\... What you've posted over the last years has caused ongoing damage to my mental health, my image, my family, my partner's mental health. I've sat silent for long enough. I know who you are.'[^17] Clinton's decision has raised many positive reviews, with some praising her for using her platform to spread awareness. However, others have criticised Clinton, suggesting that she is seeking attention by exploiting these commenters.

Clinton's approach draws attention to possible legal reforms addressing trolling on public platforms. As an influencer, the impact of trolling can be overlooked, as Australian legislation does not fully guard these individuals, leaving a risk for devastating consequences on the vulnerable when trolling goes beyond making fun of someone.[^18] Hiring a PI is an effective way to shame internet trolls, even if some do not approve of Clinton's decision. While influencers may be noticed as more exposed due to their public roles, this visibility does not justify trolling or alter the issue's seriousness.[^19] Essentially, Clinton resorted to protecting herself by exposing her trolls, which is something that has not been entirely enforced in the *OSA*.

**[Proposed Amendment in the *Online Safety Act*]{.underline}**

Even though Clinton's plan was efficient and primarily effective, this approach to all online trolling is not possible for the non-influencer individual/user. The main concerns about hiring a PI are costs and the time-consuming nature, for which Clinton confirmed that she was happy to do. Additionally, Clinton's circumstances are based on the grounds of defamation, which would not apply to the average individual. But what if not every trolling victim has the funds, time or a potential claim of defamation to pursue Clinton's approach? Besides the attempts to raise awareness provided by various third parties and the eSafety Commissioner, more action is required. [^20]

Thus, through an amendment to the *OSA*, it's suggested that social media applications, such as Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok, introduce a mandatory anti-trolling education form that each user is required to fill out to engage with the content of each application.[^21] Once signing up, users must complete the mandatory anti-trolling education form to access and enjoy the content provided by each social media service. This suggestion arose from the public's concern over trolling, in which 57% of Australians are of the view that any detection of illegal or harmful content online should be removed.[^22] Users must engage with a video that explains what trolling is, why it's bad, and when freedom of speech is considered trolling. Users are also required to acknowledge their understanding of potential consequences if trolling is detected on their account. Before finalising their login process, users must complete an engaging short questionnaire on trolling, which will determine whether they can proceed with using each desired application. From an international viewpoint, Finland's approach to incorporating digital literacy into a young person's education proves to be effective in their learning, rather than the option of banning or removing social media.[^23] This method is a reminder that not all social media is negative; it's the way that individuals utilise it.

Additionally, if the account posts derogatory terminology, through detection, their account will be suspended upon review, where users are required to fill out another mandatory anti-trolling education form. To get their account back, users will be issued a warning and are required to submit a report to the eSafety Commissioner explaining why they should've their account reinstated.[^24] The next step will remain the same process as it is, where the eSafety Commissioner would review the content and keep a report on each account for future purposes if the account again gets suspended. Though it's argued that this system is somewhat already in place through the 'Report' and 'Block' features in each application and the eSafety Commissioner's review of these accounts, this does not remove trolling; rather, it does not educate anyone and only slows down the process for a potential troller from acting again.[^25] Following Finland's approach, the proposal for better education of users is recommended for the *OSA* to amend, with scholars lobbying for this importance rather than dismissing the opportunity to incorporate this possibility into society.[^26] Though the *OSA* has developed online content regulation to a standard extent, this is not enough; therefore, through an amendment of the Act and expanding the eSafety Commissioner's roles, social media platforms can create an educational purpose to discourage trolling and harmful behaviour.[^27]

**[Takeaway]{.underline}**

Whilst individuals have the freedom to post their ideas and beliefs, it must be recognised that this privilege cannot be abused.[^28] Users may not notice the harm they are causing to others and the possible long-term consequences of their actions if they are not properly educated. Indeed, especially since the Australian Government has the power to amend the *OSA* for legislative measures to educate as well as protect anyone vulnerable.[^29] In a technology-evolving society, trolling will still be an ongoing issue; however, it's the way legal mechanisms respond to lobbying for better protection of vulnerable individuals. Thus, it's the law's responsibility to prevent trolling, protect individuals and reflect the emerging additions in content regulation.
