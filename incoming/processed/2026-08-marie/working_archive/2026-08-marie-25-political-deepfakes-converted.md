[Deepfakes and Political Disinformation]{.underline}

**Justification**

The existing subsection in the textbook on deepfakes focuses primarily on non-consensual sexual imagery in relation to deepfakes. This contribution fills a gap in the textbook by addressing political deepfakes, another harmful form of media manipulation. It demonstrates how political deepfakes threaten democracy by misleading voters and undermining trust in elections, contributing to the erosion of epistemic trust. It analyses the framework of Australian law in addressing political deepfakes, including through defamation, copyright, and online safety laws, criticising the fragmentation of this framework while contrasting it with more effective international regimes. It also highlights the logistical and constitutional challenges of regulating political deepfakes in Australia. Ultimately, it contributes to an understanding of the significant democratic risks posed by political deepfakes and the limits and challenges of legal responses.

**Overview**

While much attention has focused on sexual deepfakes, they also carry serious risks for politics, elections, and public trust. Political deepfakes, a subtype of political disinformation,[^1] have rapidly emerged as a distinct regulatory challenge. With abundant footage of politicians available on the internet,[^2] political deepfakes can threaten democracy[^3] by altering voter perceptions and eroding trust in democratic discourse.[^4]

**Democratic Risks and Emerging Evidence**

A study by Hameleers et al. found that political deepfakes can reduce trust in media and politicians,[^5] and Dobber et al. stressed that the primary threat is the "erosion of epistemic trust."[^6]

Recent data identifies 82 political deepfakes across 38 elections during 2023 and 2024.[^7] High-profile examples, such as a fabricated video of President Zelensky surrendering to Russia and a clip of Nancy Pelosi appearing inebriated while giving a political speech, illustrate how manipulated media is weaponised in politics, degrading credibility and facilitating an erosion of epistemic trust.[^8]

In the Australian context, the Australian Electoral Commission's 2025 *Stop and Consider* campaign aims to slow the spread of AI-generated political disinformation and deepfakes, but faces significant challenges given the speed and anonymity of digital manipulation.[^9]

**Australian Legal Framework**

There is no direct offence in Australian law specifically targeting political deepfakes. Such is addressed indirectly through existing common law principles and statutory provisions.

*Defamation Law*

The NSW Supreme Court in *Gilbert* recognised that digitally altered images can be defamatory, opening the door for civil liability for deepfakes under defamation.[^10] In Australia, *Voller* extended the scope of defamation liability online, recognising that platforms may be liable as publishers of defamatory content.[^11] Balancing this extended liability, 2023 defamation reforms introduced a conditional safe-harbour that shields passive service providers (such as ISPs and search engines) from liability, while allowing other hosts (such as social media platforms) to avoid liability if they provide a complaints mechanism and remove defamatory content within seven days of notice. These obligations may extend to defamatory political deepfakes, preventing legal overreach while incentivising the eradication of political disinformation online.[^12] However, while case law and recent reforms could be applied to deepfakes, neither expressly addresses them, leaving significant uncertainty.

*Copyright Law*

Under the *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth), only copyright owners or exclusive licensees can enforce infringement.[^13] This means individuals targeted by deepfakes generally lack standing, since copyright protects the economic interests of rights holders rather than the personal dignity or reputation of those depicted.[^14] However, copyright owners can still trigger takedowns through the *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) safe-harbour scheme in Pt V Div 2AA.[^15] This framework protects service providers (such as ISPs, hosting platforms, universities, and libraries) from liability for copyright infringement, provided they act on notices by removing infringing material.[^16] In practice, this means that copyright holders can compel platforms to remove deepfakes that reproduce substantial parts of their works, meaning victims may incidentally benefit when harmful deepfakes are removed. In the US, Condé Nast successfully relied on YouTube's copyright system to remove a deepfake of Kim Kardashian West, demonstrating how copyright can provide a practical mechanism for removal.[^17] However, this pathway is too indirect and incidental to provide meaningful and consistent protection for individuals targeted by political deepfakes.

*Online Safety Laws*

Online safety legislation comes closest to directly addressing political deepfakes, but its focus is restricted to intimate-image abuse. The *Online Safety Act 2021* (Cth) enables the eSafety Commissioner to order for non-consensual intimate images to be removed from sites, including altered images.[^18] The *Criminal Code Amendment (Deepfake Sexual Material) Bill 2024* goes further by criminalising sexual deepfakes and expressly codifying the term "deepfake" in Australian law.[^19] Yet despite these developments, the legislative focus remains confined to sexual harms, leaving political disinformation and other forms of manipulative content outside the regulatory framework at a time when AI manipulation is rapidly advancing.

Ultimately, the current Australian legal framework addressing political deepfakes is fragmented, necessitating broader reform through legislation that both expands coverage beyond sexual harms and directly targets political disinformation, supported by better detection tools and policy measures.[^20]

**International Approaches to Political Deepfakes**

Comparatively, the EU AI Act (2024) requires all deepfakes to be labelled, thereby covering political content, while China extends this obligation to all AI-generated media.[^21] In the United States, California and Texas legislation prohibit political deepfakes in the lead-up to elections, aimed at preventing political disinformation.[^22] These models illustrate more proactive legislative responses that expressly regulate or capture political deepfakes, in contrast with Australia's fragmented framework, where political deepfakes are unaddressed in any statutory form, and enforcement would depend on indirect mechanisms such as defamation and copyright law.

**Regulatory and Constitutional Challenges**

However, the regulation of political deepfakes involves logistical and constitutional challenges. The viral spread and extreme volume of problematic content online, as well as digital anonymity, hinder regulation, and there is the risk of "liar's dividend", where genuine footage is dismissed as fake.[^23] Moreover, regulating disinformation generally raises significant free speech concerns, prompting suggestions that reform must be measured carefully against the implied freedom of political communication to remain proportionate and tailored.[^24] For example, in US litigation during August 2025, a federal judge struck down the aforementioned Californian deepfake legislation as it was deemed to unconstitutionally burden political expression.[^25]

**Conclusion and Recommendations**

Australian law regarding political deepfakes is piecemeal. Defamation, copyright, and online safety laws may offer some remedies, but such are not designed to address political deepfakes. Overseas jurisdictions are moving faster, with laws that directly target political deepfakes. Hence, future reform must expand beyond intimate images and address political disinformation directly, while safeguarding constitutional limits on political communication. Alongside legislative change, investment in detection tools and public education will be necessary. Protecting against political deepfakes is a democratic necessity, and without it, the foundation of public trust that elections depend upon, as well as epistemic trust in knowledge itself, is at risk.
