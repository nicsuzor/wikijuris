### 6 c The ACCC's News Media Bargaining Code

With the introduction of its News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code, Australia broke new ground, establishing a legislative model that was truly the first of its kind in the world. It was crafted to restructure the intricate economic relationship between major digital platforms and the nation\'s news publishers. The Code marks a significant shift from traditional internet law; instead of focusing on intermediary liability for content, its central purpose is to correct a severe imbalance in negotiating power. This power asymmetry has historically allowed tech giants like Google and Meta to derive immense value from Australian news content without a mechanism for fairly compensating the journalists and organisations who create it.

#### Background: The Digital Platforms Inquiry

The catalyst for the Code was the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission\'s (ACCC) pivotal Digital Platforms Inquiry, which wrapped up in 2019. A key finding of the inquiry was that the market dominance of Google in search and Meta (then Facebook) in social media created a fundamental power disparity in their dealings with Australian news businesses (Australian Competition and Consumer Commission, *Digital Platforms Inquiry -- Final Report* (2019)). This lopsided dynamic left news outlets which produce the costly, high-quality journalism that drives user engagement on these platforms---with virtually no leverage to negotiate for fair payment. To remedy what it identified as a clear market failure, the ACCC\'s primary recommendation was the creation of a binding code of conduct.

#### How the Code Works: Negotiation and Arbitration

> The Code legislated through the *Treasury Laws Amendment (News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code) Act 2021* (Cth) establishes a framework to encourage commercial negotiations. It doesn\'t apply automatically to all platforms; instead the Treasurer can 'designate' a specific digital platform if it is determined that a power imbalance exists and the platform has made a significant contribution to the sustainability of the Australian news industry.

Once a platform is designated (or to avoid designation) it is required to negotiate in good faith with eligible Australian news businesses over payment for the news content made available through its services. The core enforcement mechanism and the Code\'s most innovative feature is a 'final offer arbitration' process. If the parties cannot reach a commercial agreement within three months they enter a mandatory arbitration process. Here an independent panel must choose one of the parties\' two final offers in its entirety. This model often called \"baseball arbitration\" creates a strong incentive for both sides to make reasonable and commercially realistic offers as an extreme or unreasonable offer is likely to be rejected in favour of the other party's more moderate one.

#### Impact and Outcomes: A \'Shadow\' Effect

A key measure of the Code\'s success is that no digital platform has actually been \'designated\' by the Treasurer. The mere threat of designation and the associated final offer arbitration process was sufficient to bring the major platforms to the negotiating table. In the lead-up to and following the Code\'s passage Google and Meta entered into numerous multi-million dollar licensing deals with a wide range of Australian news organisations from large players like News Corp and Nine Entertainment to smaller independent outlets (Rod Sims 'The ACCC's Digital Platform Services Inquiry and the News Media Bargaining Code' (2022) 44(2) *Sydney Law Review* 237). It is estimated that the Code has channelled over AUD \$200 million per year from platforms to local news businesses.

However the response has not been uniformly positive. In early 2021 Facebook controversially (and briefly) removed all Australian news content from its platform in protest of the proposed legislation demonstrating the significant leverage these platforms wield.

> More recently Meta has signalled it may not renew its Australian deals when they expire reflecting a broader corporate shift away from news content and raising questions about the Code\'s long-term durability (Parliament of the Commonwealth of Australia *News media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code Explanatory Memorandum* 1).

#### Global Influence and Criticism

The Australian model has been closely watched and emulated internationally. Canada passed its own version of the *Online News Act* in 2023 and similar legislative proposals have been considered in the United Kingdom, New Zealand and the United States. It has effectively created a new template for media regulation in the digital age.

Despite its successes the Code has faced criticism. Some argue it disproportionately benefits large established media corporations over smaller independent or start-up news outlets. The confidential nature of the commercial deals also creates a lack of transparency making it difficult to assess whether the funds are being used to support public interest journalism as intended (T. J. McIntyre 'Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law: The Case of Australia's News Media Code' (2021) 12 *Internet Policy Review* 4). Nonetheless the Code remains a pivotal piece of legislation in the ongoing global effort to govern the internet and ensure a sustainable future for journalism.
